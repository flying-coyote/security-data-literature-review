# Appendix M: Detection Coverage Measurement (ATT&CK→D3FEND over OCSF)

The question this appendix answers is narrow and practical: once your detections run as SQL over an OCSF-shaped store rather than as saved searches inside a SIEM, how do you know which ones actually fire on your telemetry, and how do you keep track of the difference between a detection you have written down and a detection that works. The whole appendix is organized around one distinction I keep coming back to in operational reviews, the gap between *mapped structure*, which is the design-time claim that an analytic references a technique, and *measured firing*, which is the runtime fact that the analytic returns a result over real data. Most coverage tooling reports the first and lets you believe it is the second, and a good deal of what follows is about why those two views disagree and why the disagreement is the part worth looking at.

## Section M.1: Inventory Is Not Coverage

When someone shows me an ATT&CK coverage heatmap, the first thing I want to know is what the colored cells mean, because in most tools a green cell records that some analytic in the library *references* the technique. That is design-time mapped structure (an inventory of intent), and it is a genuinely useful thing to have, but it is not the same as knowing the detection fires on your telemetry. ATT&CK Navigator makes this explicit in its own documentation, for anyone willing to take it at its word: the per-technique score field is semantically empty by design, so a green cell can mean a rule is deployed and detecting, or that the data source is merely logged, or that someone once intended to write a rule, and the tool will not tell you which. The common vendor habit is worse, because it rolls all of that up into a single coverage percentage that travels into a board deck as if it measured efficacy, when what it counts is references in a content pack.

I want to be careful not to pitch the gap as my discovery, because several people have named pieces of it well, and the framing I would defend is that I am working one level below where most of them stop. Tidal Cyber put it plainly in March 2026: coverage measures presence, not performance (Tier C, and I flag the vendor incentive, since Tidal sells coverage tooling), which is exactly the design-time/runtime line drawn at the analytic-reference level. Jared Atkinson at SpecterOps has argued for years through the "Funnel of Fidelity" and his "Capability Abstraction" work that coverage is a layer-dependent illusion: a technique you call "covered" at the procedure level may be wide open at the tool or sub-technique level, so the number depends entirely on the abstraction you measured it at (Tier B). And Anton Chuvakin keeps reminding anyone who will listen that the most common reason a detection does not fire is the unglamorous one (the collector broke, the log source silently stopped, the field you keyed on stopped populating), which is a runtime failure no inventory will surface (Tier B). The level I am adding sits underneath all three: does the OCSF class and the specific field that the analytic reads even populate in your store, and does the compiled query return a row when the attack actually happened. That is a measurement you can only make by running the detection against telemetry with known ground truth, and it is the subject of Section M.3. For the limits on what that measurement does and does not establish, see Section M.5.

## Section M.2: The Join (ATT&CK Technique → OCSF Class → D3FEND Defense)

The instrument rests on a three-part join that I find clearest to describe as two stacked views over the same chain. An offensive technique (an ATT&CK ID such as T1071) maps to whichever OCSF event class carries the telemetry the specific analytic actually reads, which in this appendix's own measurements is DNS Activity, class 4003, for the C2-domain rule scored in Section M.3, and Network Activity, class 4001, for the beacon-volume hunt walked through in Section M.4, so the binding follows the detection that reads the class and one technique can bind to more than one. That OCSF class can in turn carry a link to a D3FEND defensive technique through the schema's `d3fend` attribute. The first view is *structure*: the design-time, possibility side, which says a defense of this kind could in principle apply here. The second view is *measurement*: whether a detection written against that class actually fires on planted ground truth. The two views are built from different evidence, and the productive move is to stack them and read where they disagree, because the disagreement is the map.

The structure side is the OCSF `d3fend` object described in Section H.5.4. That object is reachable only through the `countermeasures` attribute, so the link runs from a remediation record to a D3FEND defensive technique rather than from an arbitrary event class, and the worked example there, Remediation Activity (class 7001) carrying its countermeasures, is the cleanest illustration of the link. A standards correction matters here, though, because Section H.5.4 was written against the version facts as they stood, and one of them has moved: D3FEND itself is now at version 1.4.0, released 2026-03-31 (Tier A, the published MITRE release), a point worth stating plainly since the OCSF attribute version (1.3.0) and the ontology's own version number are easy to confuse. The 1.3.0 in Section H.5.4 is correct as the OCSF *attribute* version (the `d3fend` field landed in OCSF v1.3.0 in August 2024 and carries forward through OCSF v1.8.0, the release current as of this writing in mid-2026), so keep that number where it refers to the OCSF schema, and read D3FEND-the-ontology as 1.4.0 wherever the ontology release is what is meant.

The grounding underneath that link is real but shallower than the marketing around ontologies tends to suggest, and being precise about how shallow is part of the discipline. D3FEND grounds into the Common Core Ontologies (CCO), which grounds into the Basic Formal Ontology, and BFO 2020 is ISO/IEC 21838-2:2021 (Tier A), so the documented path back toward an upper ontology is genuinely there. But when I look at the bridge file that does the grounding, the alignment is at the top of the class hierarchy, on the order of seventeen top-class axioms confirmed from the `d3fend-cco.ttl` bridge file itself:
- eight `equivalentClass` statements
- seven `rdfs:subClassOf` statements
- two property mappings

That is top-class alignment, not artifact-leaf grounding. So "D3FEND is grounded in CCO/BFO" is true as a statement about the heads of the hierarchies and is not a claim that every defensive artifact leaf inherits a formal definition, which is the same qualifier Section H.5.4 reaches for when it calls the link a reference-link path rather than a proof of formal equivalence at every leaf.

The measured side is the C5 coverage bench, which I describe in full in Section M.3. Before the numbers, the priors deserve naming, because the join is not original and I would not present it as such. MITRE ATT&CK Evals is the public instance of measured-in-lab firing I have found most rigorous, and it carries an explicit context-quality gradient in how it scores detections rather than reducing them to a binary (Tier B). MITRE's Cyber Analytics Repository (CAR) and various coverage cross-walks map analytics to techniques across rulesets, which is mapped coverage done carefully (Tier B). Atomic Red Team supplies the other half, small, reproducible tests that generate the telemetry a detection should fire on, so you can measure firing rather than infer it (Tier B). And ipurple.team has published a sensible posture of gating confidence in a detection on whether a simulation actually exercised it (Tier B). What I am claiming as the differentiator is narrower than "nobody does this": it is the OCSF-grounded join applied to *your* deployment, with the trust on every edge named rather than hidden, so that an inferred link is never quietly counted as a measured one.

## Section M.3: Measuring It (The C5 Method)

The C5 coverage bench (Tier B, synthetic testbed, single machine, never real telemetry) measures one thing and makes one claim: the runtime firing of compiled Sigma rules, run through pySigma to SQL over an OCSF-shaped fidelity store, scored against a planted APT29-style attack chain with known ground truth. The testbed is deterministic (ground-truth fingerprint `46af223bf406…`, master seed 20260601), and a re-run reproduces the result file identically, which matters because a coverage number you cannot reproduce is a number you cannot defend. Every technique lands in exactly one of three measured states. **DETECTED** means the planted truth needle is in the rule's matched set and the rule's precision clears the floor T = 0.9. **NOISY** means the needle is caught but precision falls below T, so the rule fires while dragging in benign background. **MISSED** means a recall miss, or that no rule and no needle exist for the technique, so the corpus carries no positive to catch. Those three states are the only claims the bench makes.

Across the eight techniques in this planted chain, three are detected, one is noisy, and four are missed, and I cite that 3/8 deliberately as an illustration of the method, since the absolute count is bounded by what the planted corpus happens to contain, so nobody should read it as a coverage rate to generalize from, and the transferable finding, per the bench's own results note, is the detected/noisy/missed split and the refusal to launder an inferred lead into coverage, neither of which depends on what the fraction happens to be. The per-technique results read as follows:

| Stage | ATT&CK | OCSF class | Rule | State | Matches | FPs | Precision |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| execution | T1059.001 | 1007 | encoded_powershell.yml | DETECTED | 1 | 0 | 1.0 |
| C2 | T1071 | 4003 | c2_domain.yml | DETECTED | 1 | 0 | 1.0 |
| priv-esc | T1098 | 6003 | nomfa_privesc.yml | DETECTED | 1 | 0 | 1.0 |
| lateral movement | T1021 | 4001 | rdp_lateral.yml | NOISY | 2960 | 2959 | 0.0003 |
| credential access | T1003.001 | 1007 | none | MISSED | 0 | 0 | 0.0 |
| exfiltration | T1048 | 4001 | none | MISSED | 0 | 0 | 0.0 |
| credential access | T1110 | 3002 | none | MISSED | 0 | 0 | 0.0 |
| impact | T1490 | 1007 | none | MISSED | 0 | 0 | 0.0 |

Reading the table by tactic sharpens what the headline hides. Execution, C2, and privilege escalation each come back 1/1 detected, with the specific rules (encoded PowerShell on a named host, a known C2 domain resolution, an IAM policy attach without MFA) firing cleanly at precision 1.0, which is the end-to-end evidence that detection-as-code survives the round trip from a portable Sigma rule to a query over a normalized OCSF store. Credential access comes back 0/2, with both T1003.001 and T1110 missed; exfiltration 0/1 and impact 0/1 are likewise missed. The single noisy case is the one I find most instructive, because it is the SOC false-positive tax measured rather than assumed: the RDP lateral-movement rule for T1021 catches the planted needle and every benign port-3389 connection in the store, 2,959 false positives against the one true positive, for a precision of 0.0003. A rule can only be precise about fields the OCSF normalization preserved, and the generic port-3389 signal is too coarse to separate the attack from ordinary remote-desktop traffic, which is exactly the kind of thing a measured precision column exposes and a compile-time check cannot.

The part that matters is the missed band, and the discipline there is the whole point of carrying D3FEND into the picture. For each missed technique the Security Context Graph can name D3FEND defenses that *may* counter it, carried with their provenance attached and never collapsed into the detected count. The counts of may-counter defenses are 10 for T1003.001, 26 for T1048, 25 for T1110, and 13 for T1490, seventy-four named leads in total, and across all four missed techniques, zero of them survive a min_trust = 0.6 soundness filter. Every one of those leads is an intent-blind `artifact_cooccurrence` inference at trust 0.25, meaning the defense merely shares a digital artifact with the technique, which is a place to look, not evidence that it detects anything; the caveat the bench carries in its own output puts it bluntly, that a possibility of coverage is not a measured detection and counters does not mean detects. One number from earlier drafts of this material does not survive contact with the source and I am flagging it so it does not propagate: there is no "27 zero-defense" figure in the coverage data. The supportable statement is the one the data backs directly, namely that all four missed techniques carry zero curated (min_trust ≥ 0.6) D3FEND defenses, with every named lead falling below the soundness floor.

## Section M.4: SQL Patterns

The engine that runs the M.3 measurement is SDW's own open declarative-spec layer over Sigma, OCSF, and ATT&CK, and I can describe its shape freely because it is open content end to end, and there is no commercial-engine benchmark anywhere near this section. Each detection is a small declarative spec: an ATT&CK technique and tactic, the OCSF table it runs over, a `where` predicate, a `group` key, a set of `measures`, a `having` threshold, and a `rank`. The same spec runs two ways without drift: a pure-Python pass over landed records for previews and proofs, and an emitted GROUP BY/HAVING SQL query for the live run over the Iceberg table at scale, and the reason the spec carries the aggregation rather than the Sigma rule is that the aggregation is the part Sigma cannot express. A Sigma rule alongside the spec carries only the predicate; the threshold-over-a-group is the spec's job.

The SQL shape the spec emits is the join made executable:

```sql
SELECT {bounded_group_key}, {count/sum/avg/distinct measures}
FROM   {ocsf_table}
WHERE  class_uid = {technique's OCSF class}
       AND {predicate}
GROUP BY {bounded_group_key}
HAVING {aggregate threshold}
ORDER BY {rank} DESC
LIMIT 20
```

The generalizable pattern is the chain ATT&CK technique → detection spec → OCSF class_uid → table → GROUP BY/HAVING, where the class binding lives in the `where` clause as `class_uid = N` (T1071→4001 for the beacon hunt, T1110→3002 for credential stuffing, T1490→1007 for the shadow-copy hunt, and so on), and a detection "fires" precisely when that query returns at least one aggregate row over the technique's OCSF class. The `having` clause is where the detections do work Sigma cannot: a C2 beacon is `connections >= 3 AND avg_bytes_out < 256`, exfiltration is `total_bytes_out >= 10000`, credential stuffing is `failed_attempts >= 5`, each one a threshold over a grouped aggregate rather than a per-record match.

The reason real telemetry never appears in the output is a single invariant I treat as non-negotiable, and it ties back to a discipline I hold across all of this work: real security telemetry is a prompt-injection and control-character surface, so raw rows never enter a query result that a model or a renderer will read. Every finding is an aggregate, a bounded grouping key (`src_ip`, `dst_ip`, `device_hostname`, or `user`, each allow-listed and sanitized) plus numeric measures (counts and sums). A spec that tried to group on a free-text field such as `cmd_line`, `url`, or `message` is rejected before it can run, because grouping on a free-text field would make each group a single record and surface that record's raw value as the key, a row leak disguised as an aggregate. The keyword hunts illustrate the line: a hunt for `vssadmin delete shadows` or for `lsass` *matches* the string in the predicate, but the finding it returns is a per-host count, never the command line that matched. So the SQL patterns above are the shape that lets the M.3 measurement run over real telemetry without ever putting an attacker-controlled string in front of a consumer that might act on it.

## Section M.5: Honest Limits

This is the section that governs the rest, because every capability in M.1 through M.4 carries a bounded qualifier, and I would rather state the limits plainly than let the instrument read as more than it is. There are six.

**No runtime record→defense hop**: The structure view of M.2 is a design-time link, and it does not give you a per-record path from a landed event to the defense that caught it. The OCSF `d3fend` field, where the schema carries it through to instances, rides essentially on the remediation activity path, and the deeper gap is in the ontology itself, since about 97.7% of D3FEND artifact leaves carry no OCSF link at all. That figure is SDW's own measurement, derived 2026-06-03 from the 69 reciprocal links the current spine actually carries, and I label it that way deliberately: it is not a community statistic, no public quantification of D3FEND leaf-orphan rate exists that I am aware of, and it should never be cited as anything but our own measured number.

**Coverage is measured firing, not mapped structure**: The number a coverage view should report is firing against ground truth, and the design-time D3FEND view is a statement of possibility. When the two disagree, the disagreement is the finding, and the failure mode it catches is exactly Anti-Pattern #12 ("Mapping Wrong by Construction"): a green coverage cell sitting over an OCSF field that never populates is the same defect as a vendor-shipped integration that maps the wrong events and still shows healthy counts. In both cases the only reliable signal is a measured count or firing comparison across the boundary, which is precisely what the C5 bench does, comparing measured firing against mapped structure rather than trusting the map.

**Detect-phase-only, and coverage is not efficacy**: Everything here measures whether a detection fires; it says nothing about whether firing reduces risk, and the literature on that gap is old and unkind. Axelsson's 2000 analysis of the base-rate fallacy in intrusion detection shows that even a high-accuracy detector drowns in false positives when the prior probability of attack is low, so a detection that "fires" is not a detection that helps an analyst. Sommer and Paxson's 2010 "Outside the Closed World" makes the companion point that detection faces an adversary who adapts and base rates that are genuinely difficult, which is why a measured firing rate on a fixed corpus does not transfer to efficacy in production. I cite both by name instead of gesturing at "studies," because the lit review already grounds them as Tier A, and because the reading this whole appendix supports is that it measures one phase of one part of the problem.

**Shallow CCO grounding**: As Section M.2 sets out, the D3FEND→CCO bridge is on the order of seventeen top-class axioms, top-class alignment, not leaf-level proof (Tier A, from analyzing the bridge file's own headers). So "grounded in BFO/CCO" is a documented head start in a procurement conversation, the same bounded structural advantage Section H.5.4 claims and no more, and not a guarantee that any given defensive artifact inherits a formal definition.

**Inferred, not asserted, ATT&CK↔D3FEND edges**: The matrix that connects techniques to defenses is artifact-co-occurrence inference drawn from the published 14,004-row D3FEND mappings, not a set of direct `d3f:counters` edges, and that is the reason the may-counter leads in M.3 carry trust 0.25 rather than something stronger. The inference is a reasonable place to start a hunt; it is not a statement that the defense counters the technique.

**Tier B testbed**: The whole measurement runs on one synthetic, single-machine, APT29-style chain that is never real telemetry, so the 3/8 is method-illustrative and not a universal coverage rate. What transfers is the method and the refusal to count a possibility as a detection; the specific fraction is an artifact of the planted corpus.

## Section M.6: Forward Pointers

If you want to run the measurement yourself, the C5 bench lives in the public `sdw-lab-benchmarks` repository under `ocsf-attack-coverage` (Tier B, reproducible from the seed and fingerprint above), and the live detection path is the declarative-spec engine of Section M.4, `run_detections.py`, emitting the GROUP BY/HAVING SQL over the Iceberg OCSF table using the same specs the previews run so the two cannot drift.

The natural place this appendix attaches in the main narrative is the handbook's incremental-modernization material, Chapter 7, on modularity. That chapter sorts the legacy ruleset into three tiers before any translation starts:

- the high-value fifth of the ruleset migrates first with human review,
- the middle tier gets sampled and regression-tested, and
- the dead long tail gets deleted rather than translated.

Once those rules are off the SIEM you need a way to prove the migrated detections actually fire on your OCSF store instead of assuming the translation preserved their behavior, and that proof method is this appendix. It feeds directly into the migration success metrics, where a coverage figure that reports measured firing is the kind of number that survives the six-month "was it worth it" review, in a way a count of references never is. And it is the instrument the people who own OCSF schema validation and the Great Expectations suites would run, since a measured-firing coverage check is the same class of work as the OCSF validation Appendix J.2.1 describes.

For the standing resource list (ATT&CK, D3FEND, OCSF, and the Security Context Graph that supplies the D3FEND leads), see Appendix E instead of a duplicate list here (CAR and Atomic Red Team are cited in Section M.2). And the reciprocal of this whole appendix is Section H.5.4: that section describes the OCSF `d3fend` attribute as the *structure* view, the design-time possibility of a defense applying, and this appendix is the *measured* counterpart that tests whether the detection fires. H.5.4 states the possibility; Appendix M states whether it happened, and reading the two together is how you tell mapped structure from measured firing.
